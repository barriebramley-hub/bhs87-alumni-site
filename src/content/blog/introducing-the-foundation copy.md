When I was asked to submit a piece for the Class of '87 blog focussing on beach holidays, my thoughts immediately went to the memories of many beachside holidays I’ve been fortunate enough to have had. Always having loved being at the sea, associating the ocean with comfort and contentment comes quite naturally to me.

I expect I’m not alone in how I feel about the beach and the ocean. I’m sure many of us associate the beach with happy, possibly simpler times — holidays with family and young children, long summer days with friends watching the sun set over the horizon, the sound of waves soothing away the noise in our heads, feeling the warmth of the sun rays on our bodies. There’s something timeless about the sea: it calms, it humbles, and somehow, I expect, it can offer healing too. By now, few of us have escaped the loss of something precious or someone meaningful. In recent weeks, our year group has had to say goodbye to two classmates, and over the years there have been many more. Others among us have had to bear the unbearable burden of losing beloved life partners. I’m not presuming to know how any of the people who have recently experienced personal loss are feeling. I can only reflect on my own experience of grief and loss. There are plenty of completely crippling and heartbreaking moments, but it struck me that grief shows up in quieter ways too — in letting go of what once was, in watching our parents age, or in feeling time move more quickly than we expected.

What I was reminded of is that grief doesn’t move in straight lines. It comes and goes, just like the ocean — sometimes gentle, sometimes fierce, but always changing. Just when you think you’re steady again, another wave can catch you by surprise. But in this way, the ocean serves as reminder that this rhythm is natural.

Life, just like the tide, never stays still, but it also always returns. So too does our capacity to breathe, to feel, to begin again.

There’s something grounding about the sea when grief feels heavy. The sound of waves can soothe our breathing and calm our thoughts; the salt air can clear the fog in our minds. Even the simple act of standing barefoot in the sand connects us to something solid when the world feels uncertain. The beach offers space — to remember, to cry, to be quiet — without needing to explain or perform.

And then there’s the ocean’s greatest lesson: connection. Every drop of water is part of something vast and shared — it travels, transforms, returns. Love and memory work much the same way. The people we’ve lost are never truly gone; they’re carried forward in the stories we tell, the habits we’ve inherited, the ways we’ve grown. Their presence, like the sea, is constant even when unseen.

Though most of us now live far apart, scattered across cities and continents, the ties between us — formed in those five formative years at school — still hold. We may not see each other often, but when one of us grieves, the others lean in. We reach out, we check in, we remember. There’s comfort in that, the same comfort the ocean offers: a reminder that we are all connected, and none of us faces life’s changing tides alone.

So perhaps, next time you find yourself near the water, take a moment to listen to the waves. Let them remind you that it’s okay to ebb and flow, to grieve and to heal — that both are part of the same rhythm. Like the sea, we are always moving, always returning, always whole.
