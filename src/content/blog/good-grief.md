Grief (n.), if you look up the word in the dictionary, refers to the intense sorrow experienced after someone’s death, the deeply felt distress caused by bereavement or loss, and the period of suffering.

Grief, or mourning, involves coping with loss. While primarily we refer to loss due to death, we also experience other forms of losses. Events such as divorce, loss of a home, loss of a job, loss of a pet, loss of a sense of self after a big change or trauma, loss as our children grow up and leave home – these major life events can lead to grief.

Most importantly, grief, loss, and bereavement – these are inherently human experiences. We cannot avoid these forms of suffering if we love and connect with people, places, and things. As I write this, I’m remembering (and missing) my best friend who died of cancer three years ago, on this very day. Her loss has become a key part of my life story...

We each experience grief uniquely, personally and differently, and generally enter into a process of coping and coming to terms with the loss. Loss can be anticipated (due to age or illness) or sudden; the type of loss has an effect on the grieving process. Our relationship to those who have passed is the bedrock of our grieving process. Mourning is also shaped by the rituals and customs of the religion or community we are embedded in.

However, there are common responses in grief. We tend to immediately think of Kubler-Ross, the well-known psychiatrist who described five emotional responses to loss, based on her work with terminally ill patients. These stages of grief are denial, anger, bargaining, depression, and acceptance. VERY IMPORTANT to understand that these are emotional responses, not stages that unfold in a linear, neat manner. Grief is messy and complicated.
Some people experience them all, skip some feelings, or even repeat the feeling processes.

Some people even experience periods of intense psychic numbing that can persist for several months, as they face the enormity of their loss.

So, we know that grief does not have stages, like a step-by-step programme. What does that mean for healing and coping?

I suggest, above all, patience and compassion, for ourselves and others. Allow the feelings to flow. A lovely model of grief, developed by Worden (2009), speaks of the tasks of grieving, without prescribed actions or time limits. These four tasks are described as follows:

1. To accept the reality of the loss (in others words, integrating the reality of the death or loss into your whole being, body, mind and soul);
2. To process the pain of grief (experiencing it on all levels, emotionally, cognitively, physical and spiritually, and letting these feeling experiences flow and be expressed);
3. To adjust to a world without the loved one (externally, taking time to learn to live without the person in your world, as well as internally as you develop a new sense of self);
4. To find an enduring connection with the loved one in the midst of embarking on a post-loss life (creating a balance between remembering and living a full, meaningful life).

Above all, we are human and need to experience the feelings and fears, our losses and pain as well as the joy and wonder of life. It’s not always easy. Patience, with ourselves and others, and connection, with family friends, supportive others, are key factors that can sustain us during grief. And to let our emotional responses flow, to come and go like the waves in the ocean.

That said, it’s not easy or pleasant at times. Sometimes we resort to stuckness, denial and self-harming, self-medicating.
Surely, there is no way to process grief in an easy/good manner. We feel the loss deeply. But with emotional awareness, and self-compassion, we can persist. We can make it through grief... and as we commit to feeling and expressing our internal world, we can process the hurt and sadness as we remember and rebuild.

Worden, J.W. (2009). Grief counselling and grief therapy: A handbook for the mental health practitioner (4 th ed.), Springer.
